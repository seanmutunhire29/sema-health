1. Dehydration and Diarrhea
The goal is to replace lost fluids and electrolytes. If you don't have a pre-made packet, you can make a DIY Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS).
The Recipe:
* Boil and cool 1 Liter of clean water.
*  6 level teaspoons of sugar.
*  1/2 level teaspoon of salt.
Procedure: Stir until dissolved. Sip slowly and frequently.
Note: If the patient cannot keep fluids down or shows signs of extreme lethargy, seek medical help immediately.


2. Minor Wounds (Cuts and Scrapes)
The priority here is stopping the bleed and preventing infection.
*  Clean your hands: Use soap or sanitizer first.
*  Stop the bleeding: Apply gentle pressure with a clean cloth or bandage.
*  Clean the wound: Rinse with cool, clear water. Avoid using harsh hydrogen peroxide or iodine directly in the wound as they can damage tissue; mild soap on the surrounding skin is fine.
*  Apply an antibiotic: A thin layer of antibiotic ointment or petroleum jelly(vaseline) keeps the surface moist.
*  Cover it: Use a sterile bandage to keep bacteria out.


3. Severe Bleeding
* Call for help: Dial emergency services immediately.
* Direct Pressure: Push down hard on the wound with both hands using a clean cloth. Do not lift it to "check" if it stopped.
* Tourniquet (If necessary): If it’s a limb and pressure isn't working, apply a tourniquet 2-3 inches above the wound (between the wound and the heart).


4. Headaches
Most headaches aren't emergencies, but they are a literal pain.
* Tension/Dehydration: Drink a glass of water, rest in a dark room, or use a cool compress on the forehead.
*  Medication: Over-the-counter options like Ibuprofen or Acetaminophen usually help.
*  When to worry: If the headache is the "worst of your life," follows a head injury, or comes with a stiff neck and fever, go to the ER.


5. Burns (Minor)
* Cool it down: Run cool (not cold) tap water over the burn for about 10–20 minutes.
* Remove jewelry: Do this before the area starts to swell.
* Don't pop blisters: This increases the risk of infection.
* Cover loosely: Use a sterile gauze bandage.


6. Choking (The Heimlich Maneuver)
If the person can cough or speak, encourage them to keep coughing. If they cannot breathe:
* Stand behind them: Wrap your arms around their waist.
* Make a fist: Place the thumb side of your fist just above their navel.
* Thrust: Perform quick, upward and inward thrusts until the object is forced out.


7. Choking (Infants < 1 year)
The Heimlich maneuver is for adults/children. For babies, the technique is different:
* 5 Back Blows: Lay the baby face-down on your forearm, resting on your thigh. Support the jaw. Give 5 firm blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
* 5 Chest Thrusts: Turn the baby face-up. Use two fingers in the center of the chest (just below the nipple line) and give 5 quick thrusts.
* Repeat: Continue until the object comes out or the baby starts crying.


8. Sprains and Strains (The R.I.C.E. Method)
* Rest: Avoid putting weight on the injured area. 
* Ice: Apply cold packs for 15-20 minutes every few hours.
* Compression: Wrap the area with an elastic bandage (not too tight!). 
* Elevation: Keep the injured limb above the level of the heart. 


9. Severe Allergic Reactions (Anaphylaxis)
* This is a medical emergency where the airway can close. Look for swelling of the lips/tongue, hives, or difficulty breathing.
* Call Emergency Services: Do this immediately.
* Use an EpiPen: If the person has an emergency epinephrine autoinjector, help them use it. Inject into the outer mid-thigh.
* Positioning: Have the person lie flat on their back with their feet raised (unless they are vomiting or having trouble breathing, in which case, let them sit up).
* Second Dose: If symptoms don't improve and help is more than 5–10 minutes away, a second injection may be needed.


10. Snake Bites
The goal is to keep the venom from spreading through the bloodstream.
* Safety First: Move away from the snake's striking distance. Do not try to catch or kill it.
* Keep Still and Calm: This is the most important step. Keeping the heart rate low slows the spread of venom.
* Positioning: Keep the bitten limb below the level of the heart.
* Remove Jewelry: Rings and watches should come off before the limb swells.
* Clean and Cover: Wash the bite gently with soap and water and cover with a clean, dry dressing.
WHAT NOT TO DO
* Do NOT use a tourniquet.
* Do NOT cut the wound or try to suck out the venom (that only happens in movies and actually makes it worse).
* Do NOT apply ice.


11. Poisoning (Ingestion)
Whether it’s household chemicals or the wrong medication, speed is key.
* Identify the Substance: If possible, find the container or bottle to tell the medical professionals exactly what was swallowed.
* Call Poison Control: Contact your local poison control center or emergency services immediately.
* Do NOT induce vomiting: Unless a medical professional specifically tells you to. Some chemicals (like bleach) can cause more damage coming back up.
* Clear the mouth: If the person swallowed a pill or solid, try to clear any remaining pieces from their mouth.


12.  Heat Stroke
This happens when the body's internal temperature rises above 40°C (104°F).
* Move to Shade: Get the person into a cool or air-conditioned environment.
* Cool Rapidly: This is a race. Spray them with cool water, use fans, or place ice packs on the neck, armpits, and groin.
* Hydrate (if conscious): Give small sips of cool water or an electrolyte drink.
* Monitor: If they lose consciousness, turn them on their side (recovery position) and wait for paramedics.


13. Seizures
* Clear the Space: Move hard or sharp objects away from the person.
* Protect the Head: Place something soft (like a folded jacket) under their head.
* Time it: Note when the seizure started. If it lasts longer than 5 minutes, call for help.
* Do NOT Restrain: Never hold the person down or try to stop their movements.
* Do NOT put anything in their mouth: It is a myth that people can swallow their tongues; putting objects in their mouth can cause choking or broken teeth.


14. Nosebleeds
* Sit Upright: Do not lie down.
* Lean Forward: Lean forward slightly so blood goes out the nose rather than down the throat (which can cause gagging or vomiting).
* Pinch the Soft Part: Use your thumb and index finger to pinch the soft part of the nose shut for 10 full minutes without letting go.
* Ice: You can place an ice pack on the bridge of the nose to help constrict blood vessels.


15. Bee Stings & Insect Bites
* Remove the Stinger: If it’s still there, scrape it out with a flat object like a credit card. Do not use tweezers, as squeezing the stinger can inject more venom.
* Wash: Use soap and water.
* Cold Compress: Apply ice to reduce swelling.
* Elevation: If the sting is on an arm or leg, keep it raised.
* Watch for Allergy: If the person develops hives or trouble breathing, refer to the Anaphylaxis section above.


16. Objects in the Eye
* Don't Rub: Rubbing can scratch the cornea.
* Blink: Sometimes natural tearing will wash the object out.
* Flush: Use a gentle stream of clean water or saline solution. Tilt the head so the affected eye is down (to avoid washing the object into the other eye).
* Cover and Seek Help: If the object is embedded in the eye, do not touch it. Place a paper cup over the eye to protect it and go to the ER.


17. Fainting
* Lay them Flat: If they feel faint, have them lie down. If they have already fainted, keep them on their back.
* Elevate Legs: Raise their feet about 12 inches above heart level to get blood back to the brain.
* Check Breathing: If they don't regain consciousness within a minute, call emergency services.
* Fresh Air: Loosen tight clothing and ensure the area isn't crowded.


18. Frostbite & Hypothermia
* Get to Warmth: Move the person out of the cold.
* Dry Clothes: Replace wet clothing with dry blankets.
* Warm Slowly: For frostbit skin (fingers/toes), soak in lukewarm (not hot!) water.
* No Rubbing: Never rub frostbit skin; the ice crystals in the tissue can cause more damage.
* Warm Drinks: Give warm, non-alcoholic liquids.


19. Electric Shock
* Do Not Touch: If the person is still in contact with the electrical source, do not touch them or you will be shocked too.
* Turn off Power: Shut off the breaker or unplug the device. If you can't, use a non-conducting object (like a wooden broom handle) to push the person away from the source.
* Check Vital Signs: Once safe, check for breathing. Even if they look fine, internal damage can occur; they should see a doctor.


20. Dental Emergencies (Knocked-Out Tooth)
* Handle with Care: Pick up the tooth by the crown (the chewing part), not the root.
* Rinse: If dirty, rinse gently with water. Do not scrub it.
* Re-insert: If possible, try to put the tooth back in the socket and have the person bite down on gauze.
* Storage: If it won't go back in, put it in a small container of milk or the person's own saliva. See a dentist within 30 minutes.


21. Broken Limbs (Fractures)
The main goal is to immobilize the area to prevent further damage to nerves, blood vessels, and tissues.
* Stop any bleeding: Apply pressure with a clean cloth.
* Immobilize: Do not try to "straighten" the bone. Use a splint (folded cardboard, a heavy stick, or a rolled-up magazine) to keep the joint above and below the break from moving.
* Apply Ice: Wrap ice in a cloth and apply it to the area to reduce swelling and pain.
* Treat for Shock: Have the person lie down and keep them warm with a blanket.
* Seek Help: Every suspected broken bone needs an X-ray.


22. Fever
A fever is usually the body’s way of fighting infection, but it needs to be managed for comfort and safety.
* Hydrate: Encourage water, juice, or broth.
* Comfortable Clothing: Use light clothing. Piling on blankets can actually trap heat and raise the temperature further.
* Medication: Use Acetaminophen or Ibuprofen as directed. Avoid Aspirin for children due to the risk of Reye’s syndrome.
* Lukewarm Sponge Bath: This can help lower temperature, but stop if the person starts to shiver (shivering raises internal body temp).
* When to see a doctor:
   * An adult with a fever over 39.4°C (103°F).
   *    An infant under 3 months with any fever.
   *    If the fever is accompanied by a stiff neck, confusion, or a rash.


23. Skin Itch (Rashes, Hives, or Poison Ivy)
* Identify the Trigger: If it’s a plant (Poison Ivy/Oak), wash the skin immediately with soap and water to remove the oils.
* Cool Compress: Apply a cool, damp cloth to the itchy area.
* Anti-Itch Creams: Use Hydrocortisone cream or Calamine lotion.
* Oral Antihistamines: OTC medications (like Benadryl) can help if the itch is widespread or caused by an allergy.
* Don't Scratch: Scratching can break the skin and lead to a bacterial infection.


24. Splinters
* Clean: Wash your hands and the area with soap and water.
* Sterilize: Use rubbing alcohol to clean a pair of tweezers.
* Pull: Grasp the end of the splinter and pull it out at the same angle it entered.
* Deep Splinters: If it's completely under the skin, sterilize a needle and gently nick the skin over the end of the splinter to lift it out.
* Ointment: Apply antibiotic ointment after it's out.


25. Blisters (Friction)
*  Leave it alone: If the blister is intact, do not pop it. It is a natural "sterile bandage."
* Protect: Cover it loosely with a bandage or a "donut" shaped piece of moleskin.
* If it breaks: Clean with mild soap and water, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover with a bandage.


26. Sunburn
* Cool the skin: Use cool compresses or take a cool bath.
* Moisturize: Use lotions containing Aloe Vera or soy.
* Hydrate: Drink extra water to prevent dehydration.
* No Peeling: Don't pick at peeling skin; let it fall off naturally.


27. Hyperventilation (Rapid Breathing)
* Reassure: Stay calm and tell the person they are safe.
* Breathe with them: Encourage them to take slow, deep breaths, inhaling through the nose and exhaling through pursed lips (like blowing out a candle).
* The "Paper Bag" Trick: This is no longer the primary recommendation. Instead, have them cup their hands over their mouth and nose and breathe into them.


28. Strains vs. Sprains (The "Tear")
* Sprain: Injury to a ligament (bone to bone).
* Strain: Injury to a muscle or tendon (muscle to bone).
* Procedure: Treat both with the R.I.C.E. method (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) as mentioned in the previous sections.
